When Measuring Success Causes Failure

The trouble with KPIs—and what happens when the measure becomes the mission.

A STUDY OF METRICS, INCENTIVES AND UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES

A customer walks into a bank to open a savings account. An employee helps them complete the paperwork, explains the available services, and gets them set up. An ordinary banking interaction. But imagine that the employee has a target to meet before the end of the day—not a target for customer satisfaction, but a target for the number of financial products sold.

When the numbers became the objective

For years, Wells Fargo promoted a strategy built around cross-selling: encouraging existing customers to use multiple products and services offered by the bank. The business logic was understandable. A customer who uses several services may have a deeper relationship with the bank.

But employees experienced intense pressure to meet sales quotas. In September 2016, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau announced enforcement action after finding that employees had secretly opened deposit and credit card accounts without customers' knowledge or consent. Wells Fargo's analysis identified more than two million accounts that may not have been authorised by customers. Some customers incurred fees; others had their credit affected.

In February 2020, the US Department of Justice announced a $3 billion resolution of criminal and civil investigations relating to sales practices between 2002 and 2016.

How did an organisation come to treat the creation of more accounts as evidence of a stronger customer relationship?

Sources: CFPB, 2016; US Department of Justice, 2020; US Senate hearing, 2016.

When the numbers say everything is fine

Organisations routinely use measurable indicators to represent outcomes that are harder to measure. We count sales to understand customer relationships, attendance to understand service availability, examination scores to understand learning, and transactions to understand whether public services are reaching people. These measures are useful. But a measure is a representation of reality, not reality itself.

The worker whose wages had been paid—but never received

In 2017, Scroll documented the case of a MGNREGA worker identified by the pseudonym Anita Devi in Chhattisgarh's Surguja district. She had worked four weeks and was owed ₹4,454. The system showed payment had been made, but the money had gone into someone else's bank account after an Aadhaar-linking error. On the system, the transaction was complete. In her life, it was not.

Source: Scroll, April 2017.

Attendance is not healthcare

A Karnataka evaluation of biometric monitoring across 322 primary health centres in five districts found that attendance increased among medical staff but had virtually no effect on doctors. The intervention was nevertheless associated with improvements in antenatal care and infant health, including birth weight. This resists a simple success-or-failure story: healthcare is not delivered simply because someone registers their arrival.

Source: J-PAL, Karnataka biometric attendance evaluation.

When learning becomes a number

A 2021 research paper comparing India's National Achievement Survey and ASER data found significant discrepancies and cautioned against using NAS for comparisons or measuring improvement over time. This is not evidence of manipulation; it is a warning that a precise-looking number may not reliably represent the thing being measured.

Source: Research paper, 2021.

But are targets always the problem?

It would be easy to conclude that targets and performance indicators are inherently harmful. That would be the wrong lesson. India's Swachh Bharat Mission shows how ambitious goals, political commitment, communication and administrative mobilisation can help focus action. But constructing toilets, reporting coverage and ensuring sustained use of safe sanitation are related but different outcomes. Even when targets help drive progress, measurement must reflect what is happening on the ground.

Source: BMJ Global Health, 2019.

The problem with proxies

We create proxies because complex outcomes cannot always be reduced to a single number. We measure products sold, payments processed, attendance recorded, scores achieved and toilets constructed. A proxy is not inherently bad. The danger is assuming that improving the proxy necessarily improves the outcome.

This is sometimes called Goodhart's law, often summarised as: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” The point is not that every target will inevitably be manipulated. It is that the relationship between a measure and the reality it represents can change once people begin optimising for the measure itself.

A familiar problem in software development

Software quality is sometimes measured through test cases written, cases executed, bugs reported or closed, and execution percentages. These are useful activity indicators, but none alone tells us whether a product is reliable or customers are having a good experience. Teams need to interpret them alongside escaped defects, severity, risk coverage, production reliability and customer impact.

The question we should have asked

The original business ambition at Wells Fargo was not unreasonable. A bank wants customers to use its services. But the number of products became a substitute for the quality of that relationship.

What meaningful outcome is this number supposed to represent?

What behaviour will this measurement encourage? What might people do to improve the number without improving the outcome? Who could be disadvantaged if the system optimises for this target? What evidence would tell us that the real-world problem has actually improved?

The most dangerous thing about a misleading metric is not necessarily that it produces a wrong number. It is that it can produce a very convincing number—and persuade an entire organisation that it is succeeding.

Perhaps the first step towards better measurement is not finding a better KPI. Perhaps it is questioning whether we are measuring the right problem at all.
